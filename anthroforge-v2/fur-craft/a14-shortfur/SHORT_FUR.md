# A14 — Short Fur & Fuzz: the sub-card toolkit

**Question:** muzzles, chins, cheeks, ear backs and paws carry fur too short for the
card pipeline (A-wave fur prototype: cards start paying off around 15–20 mm). What
renders those zones? This study builds the three production answers side by side on
the neutral base head v3, in one shared muzzle+cheek zone, and judges them at close
and medium distance.

**Prototype:** `shortfur_compare.blend` — four copies of the v3 head (BASE control +
T1/T2/T3), one zone mask, one studio rig. Renders in `renders/`, build script in
`work/build_shortfur.py`, pelt textures generated by `work/gen_textures.py`.

---

## 1. The zone (the control that makes the comparison honest)

All three techniques are applied to the *same* mask, baked once as a vertex-colour
attribute (`A14Zone`) on TissueHead + Jaw from world-space ellipsoids: muzzle block,
both cheeks (masseter + zygomatic), chin front; nose leather and eyes carved out,
feathered edges. Verified visually in `renders/debug_zone_mask.png`. Outside the
zone, every head wears the identical flat coat material — so any visible difference
inside the zone is the technique, not the paint job. Palette is one tawny coat
(root `#3d2d1d` → base `#7d6248` → tip `#a5855e`) shared by all three.

## 2. The three techniques

### T1 — Velvet / fresnel fuzz (shader only, zero geometry)
Short fur's biggest *silhouette* cue is not strands — it's the soft halo where
thousands of hair tips scatter light at grazing angles. Physically this is a
retro-reflective sheen lobe (Ashikhmin-style velvet distribution; The Order: 1886's
cloth BRDF work identifies rim fuzz from asperity scattering as *the* distinguishing
feature of velvet-like materials, and production sheen implementations explicitly
list "peach fuzz / fine hair" as a target use). Implementation: Principled BSDF with
**Sheen Weight driven by the zone** (0.15 off-zone → 1.0 in-zone), Sheen Roughness
0.5, warm sheen tint, plus a Layer-Weight fresnel ramp that lightens the base colour
toward a pale fuzz tint at grazing view angles, zone-masked. Cost: no geometry, no
textures, one material. Weakness by construction: it cannot produce *any* surface
detail at close range — the muzzle stays as smooth as the underlying 962-vert mesh;
it only changes how light wraps it.

### T2 — Shell micro-layers (geometry ×14, shader alpha)
Shell texturing (Lengyel et al. 2001; Shadow of the Colossus; Xenoblade's instanced
shells; Stray's cat wears a shell "furmesh") stacks copies of the mesh offset along
normals, each textured with a strand pattern whose density thins with height — the
eye integrates the stack into a volume. Micro version for short coats: **14 shells
over 0.012 units ≈ 3 mm** of coat (head L = 1.0 ≈ 25 cm), strand pattern from a 3D
object-space noise (Kajiya–Kay style volumetric strands: a column of noise survives
across layers, so a "hair" is one column seen at 14 heights), keep-threshold rising
0.20 → 0.80 from root to tip, root→tip colour ramp per layer, zone-masked alpha,
dark root material underneath. Geometry cost here: 21,000 shell verts for two head
parts — trivial; the real cost is transparent overdraw (14 layers) and transparent
bounce depth in path tracing. Published practice for short/spiky shell fur lands at
~12 layers for a convincing read (Kai Klein's AAA-fur thesis, Blender workflow), so
14 is in-family, not padded.

### T3 — Texture-only directional pelt (zero geometry, 3 maps)
The oldest trick and still the base layer of most shipped animals: a *directional*
fur texture — thousands of short strokes along a flow direction, root-dark →
tip-light, guard-hair speckle — carried in albedo, with a matching **normal map**
(Sobel-derived from the stroke height field) and roughness variation. Here the maps
are procedural (`work/gen_textures.py`: 11,000 jittered strokes, tileable, 1024²)
because the v3 head's UVs come from its source avatar and no painted pelt exists for
it. Production version of this technique paints the maps (or generates them in
Substance) *and* pairs them with a flow/direction map — Autodesk Stingray's standard
fur material is exactly this family (noise map + length mask + direction map +
normal scale), and its docs state the production rule this study confirms: where
fur length → 0, normal-mapped pelt is what carries the detail. Weakness: no
silhouette effect at all, and it is hostage to UV quality/flow direction.

## 3. Verdict — judged on the renders

Judged on: `renders/montage_closeups.png` (four close-ups, same camera/light),
`renders/render_grazing_t2.png` (shells in profile), `renders/lineup_labelled.png`
(all four at medium distance; screen order T3 · T2 · T1 · BASE). Studio light
(flat world + key/fill/rim areas) — the v3 Poly Haven HDRI world was replaced
after measurement showed it costing ~4.5× per sample in this scene.

**Close-up (muzzle + cheek fills frame)**
- **BASE** — flat tawny clay. Zero fur read; the base head's low-poly faceting
  shows on cheek and jaw. This is what "no fur system" actually looks like.
- **T1 velvet** — ≈ BASE. A touch lighter/warmer, the broad cheek highlight
  marginally softer, and that is all. From a frontal camera the fresnel term
  has almost nothing to bite on, and sheen adds no surface detail. *As
  predicted: T1 is not a coat.*
- **T3 pelt** — the first frame that reads as *fur*: directional stubble
  grain, root→tip value variation, normal relief catching the key light.
  Debits, honestly: in the hardest light the relief tips toward
  carved-wood/bark (normal strength 0.85 is too much — halve it in
  production), and on the muzzle the flow breaks into chevrons where the
  source UV direction turns — flow follows UVs, not anatomy, exactly the
  documented risk. Zone-edge feathering is clean.
- **T2 shells** — the only frame with real *volume*: a dense plush pile with
  depth, root-to-tip shading, and a soft broken silhouette. Also the only
  frame that reads slightly wrong for a muzzle: directionless noise cells
  (~2–3 mm) read as terrycloth/carpet tufts rather than strands, there is a
  pale halo fringe where the stack ends at the jaw silhouette, and black
  patches appear where rays exhaust the transparent-bounce budget.

**Grazing / profile (T2)**
- The silhouette is genuinely fuzzy and the pile read survives grazing
  incidence (no collapse to a shiny sheet). No birthday-cake banding at 14
  layers — a slight layered fringe at the cheek's back edge reads as fuzz,
  not steps. Debits: black shard artifacts near the ear base, and the
  *unzoned* ear (bare low-poly mesh) looks jarringly naked next to the
  coated head — accidental but useful proof that in production the ears
  need at least T3/T1 even when the study zone excludes them.

**Medium distance (lineup)**
- **T3 still reads.** The grain survives at distance; the head looks coated
  next to BASE's clay. Best read-per-cost in the test, by a wide margin.
- **T2 fails at this distance in this build.** Dark blotchy patches,
  semi-transparent-looking areas, ears that look detached. The alpha stack
  + 20 samples + denoise smear produces a dirty, damaged read — a real
  distance-dependent failure mode (the real-time equivalent is
  sorting/dither noise), not a tuning nit.
- **T1 ≈ BASE** in neutral studio light. Its rim contribution needs a
  deliberate backlight to exist at all.

**Measured cost** (Cycles, this VM, thread-capped studio pass, 720² close-ups)
— T3 close 168 s · T2 close 752 s (≈4.5× T3) · T2 grazing 876 s · full
four-head lineup only 151 s because shells cover few pixels at distance:
shell cost is pixel-coverage-driven. Geometry: T1/T3 add none; T2 adds
21,000 verts and 14× transparent overdraw inside the zone. T3's cost is
3× 1024² maps.

**Verdict — the production stack for short coats**
1. **T3 pelt is the default base** for every coat under ~10 mm: muzzle,
   cheeks, paws, ears, short-coated species. Directional grain + root/tip
   maps, normal strength ~0.4, flow painted to anatomy (not raw UVs).
2. **T1 sheen rides on top of T3** wherever a rim/backlight exists — nearly
   free, and it is garnish, never the coat itself.
3. **T2 shells are a hero-asset option, not a default**: plush 6–15 mm
   coats at close range only, with the sample/bounce budget to feed them,
   accepting the medium-distance failure above. In real-time targets use
   hashed/dithered alpha instead of sorted transparency.
4. Bare leather (nose, paw pads) and the eye exclusion stay bare under all
   three — the zone mask, not the technique, owns that boundary.

## 4. Coat length → technique table

Lengths are guard-hair lengths on the real animal; "close" ≈ portrait distance in
the creator app, "medium" ≈ full-head framing. Below ~15 mm, cards lose: a card is
a *plane*, and a 5 mm plane reads as a spike, not a hair (the fur prototype's card
half already showed budget starvation at body scale; at muzzle scale it's worse).

| Coat length | Where it occurs | Technique | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (bare) | nose leather, paw pads, eye rims | none — skin/leather material | roughness + bump only; any fur system must mask OUT (our zone does) |
| < 1 mm | whisker pads, chin nub | T3 texture-only | reads as skin grain; whiskers are separate geometry, not coat |
| 1–4 mm stubble | sleek muzzle, chin, paw tops (short-coated breeds/species) | **T3** (+ T1 if backlit portraits matter) | shells alias into sparkle at this length; normal map does the work |
| 3–10 mm short coat | **face, cheeks, ears, paws of most mammals — this study's zone** | **T1 + T3 paired** | T3 gives close-up grain, T1 gives silhouette softness; either alone is visibly missing the other's half |
| 6–15 mm, camera close | plush cheeks (British Shorthair type), bear face | **T2 shells over T3 base** | shells earn their overdraw only when the camera is close enough to see layer depth; T1 still welcome at the rim |
| 15–40 mm | neck, chest, body short-medium | T2 at LOD-near → cards at LOD-mid | crossfade by distance, same region maps as the groom |
| 40 mm + | ruff, tail, mane | cards / strands (fur prototype pipeline) | — |

Per-zone defaults for the creator's head/body presets:

| Zone | Default stack |
|---|---|
| Nose leather, lips, paw pads | bare material |
| Muzzle + chin | T3, T1 light (sheen 0.5) |
| Cheeks | T1 + T3; T2 if species coat ≥ plush |
| Ear back | T1 (+ T3 if UVs behave) |
| Ear inner | texture wisps / a few cards — never shells (concavity bands badly) |
| Paw dorsal | T3 |
| Paw between toes | T3 darkened (AO trap zone) |
| Forehead/dome | T1 + T3 (flows into cheek settings) |

## 5. Rules this establishes for the pipeline

1. **Short fur is a two-layer answer.** Every production reference that ships
   close-up animals pairs a shading trick with a texture trick (Stingray: shells +
   normal detail where shells end; Stray: furmesh over textured body). Budget T1+T3
   as one line item, not alternatives.
2. **The zone/length mask is the shared currency.** The same mask that feathers
   shells here should drive groom length in the strand pipeline (the British
   Shorthair groom in VFX Grace's Blender Conference work is shaped exactly this
   way: a painted texture trims muzzle length to define the structure). Author
   masks once, consume everywhere.
3. **Fuzz is the cheapest silhouette fix we own.** If a head reads "plastic" at
   medium distance, sheen is a one-material change — try it before touching
   geometry.
4. **Shells are a close-up luxury, not a default.** 14 layers ≈ 14× overdraw on
   the zone in real-time targets; gate them behind camera distance / quality tier,
   and never on concave zones (inner ear) where the layers band.
5. **T3 is only as good as its flow.** Procedural strokes prove the shading model;
   production pelts need painted flow (direction) maps per species — the muzzle
   flow (radiating from the nose, sweeping back over the cheeks) is anatomy, and a
   generic vertical flow will read wrong on a real character even when the
   statistics are right.

## 6. Honest limits of this prototype

- The v3 head is 962 tissue verts; at close-up, all three techniques sit on visible
  faceting that no shader removes. Verdicts are about the fur read, not the mesh.
- T3's flow follows the source avatar's UVs, not anatomy (see rule 5) — its
  close-up is a *best case for statistics, worst case for direction*.
- T2's noise is object-space 3D noise; strand columns don't lean with a groom
  direction. At 3 mm the lean is sub-visible; at 10 mm+ it wouldn't be.
- Cycles path-traced transparency is kinder to shells than a real-time alpha-hash
  pipeline will be; the overdraw warning in rule 4 stands regardless.
